Heuristic Filter Set 1 ­ The Gopher Rules

(Document-Level Statistics)

· Origin: Developed by DeepMind for the Gopher model (2021) on the MassiveText dataset.

· Rules (ALL must pass -- any failure = document discarded):


· Word count: 50  len(words)  100,000 -- removes single-line fragments and book-length spam.


· Mean word length: 3  ¯w  10 chars -- removes tokenizer artifacts and garbled text.


· Alpha-word ratio: r = | {w : w contains letter} | /N  0.8 -- removes symbol-heavy tables and code.


·  Symbol-word ratio: rs =  #(#) + #(...)   0.1 -- removes hashtag-spam and snippet pages.

                                 N

· Line rules:  90% of lines start with a bullet;  30% of lines end with "..." -- removes list/feed pages.


· Stop-word rule: must contain  2 of {the, be, to, of, and, that, have, with} -- removes non-prose.


· Repetition rules: a family of ~10 thresholds -- duplicate line and paragraph fractions, and the character

   share taken by the most frequent or repeated n-grams (e.g. duplicate line fraction  0.30). These, not the

   rules above, catch a page that repeats one sentence twenty times.


· Language caveat: "words" are whitespace tokens. For Thai, segment words rst (e.g., PyThaiNLP) and re-

   tune every threshold (word count, mean length, stop-word rules) on Thai text -- English values do not transfer. 11

Heuristic Filter Set 2 ­ The C4 Rules

(Line-Level & Pattern Cleaning)

· Origin: C4 cleaning rules (Ra el et al. 2020, §2.2) prepared the training data for T5

   (Week 8).


· Line-level rules: keep only lines ending in terminal punctuation (. ! ? or closing quote);

   drop lines with fewer than 3 words; drop any line containing the word "Javascript".


· Page-level rules: drop pages with fewer than 5 sentences, pages containing "lorem

   ipsum", pages containing a curly bracket `{`, and pages with words from a bad-word
   list.


· Deduplication rule: keep only one copy of any three-sentence span that occurs more

   than once in the corpus.


· Language rule: keep pages `langdetect` labels English with probability  0.99.


· Caution: These rules also remove valid code, maths (`{` in LaTeX), poetry, and non-

   English text; audit samples.

Web-Scale Deduplication ­ Why It Matters

· Problem: Syndication and copied pages create

   exact and near duplicates.


· Memorization Risk: Repeated examples increase 

   verbatim recall and privacy concerns.


· Bias Risk: Frequent duplicates receive disproportionate training weight.

· Compute Cost: Reprocessing copies wastes tokens and training steps.

· Exact Dedup: Hash normalized documents to nd identical copies.

· Near Dedup: Use shingles, MinHash, and LSH to generate candidates.

· Report: Measure retention on your corpus; do not assume a universal

   percentage.

Exact Deduplication ­ SHA256 Hashing

· Method: Compute the SHA-256 cryptographic hash of the normalised document text; store seen hashes in a

   hash set.


· Algorithm:

   · 1. Normalise text: strip whitespace, lowercase, remove HTML artefacts.

   · 2. Compute the digest: .hexdigest()  64-char string when you want to read it; .digest()  the same

       256 bits as 32 packed bytes when you want to store it (see Storage below).


   · 3. Look up hash in seen set: if present  duplicate  discard; else  add to set and keep.

· Complexity: Hashing = O(|D|); lookup = O(1) amortised (hash set).

· Collision probability:  2-256 for a given pair; across n documents the any-pair probability is  n(n - 1)/2257.

   Both are negligible at web scale.


· Limitation: Only catches exact matches (byte-for-byte identical after normalisation). Di erent headline, same

   article = missed duplicate.


· Storage: a packed digest is 32 bytes, so 40B docs  1.28 TB of raw digest payload -- a lower bound.

   `hexdigest()` returns 64 characters, and any in-memory set adds per-object overhead on top. Manageable with
   distributed key-value stores (Redis, DynamoDB).

The Cost of Deduplication ­ Two Axes

· Total work = (number of pairs) × (cost per pair). Each technique attacks a di erent factor --

   that is why they stack instead of competing.


· Two independent axes. s = shingles per document, N = documents in the corpus. A bigger
  corpus does not make each document longer, so brute force is N2s2 -- never N3.


· MinHash cuts the rst column (17,689 -> 64 for the demo pair) and leaves the second

   untouched. LSH cuts the second.


· At N = 40B the second column is 8 × 1020: at 64 comparisons each, MinHash alone still runs for

   millennia. LSH is not an optimisation -- it is what makes the job nite.

· Core Idea: Instead of counting matching tokens, embed EVERY token of both candidate and

   reference into contextual vector space, then measure similarity in embedding space.


· Step 1 -- Embed: Feed candidate c = (c1, ..., cn) and reference r = (r1, ..., rm) through a pre-

   trained BERT model.


  · Candidate embeddings: C  n×d, Reference embeddings: R  m×d


· Step 2 -- Similarity Matrix: Compute pairwise cosine similarity:

            ci  rj

· Key Insight: Sij measures how semantically similar token ci is to token rj -- captures paraphrase

   automatically.

Evaluation Flaw I ­ Length Bias

· Observation (Zheng et al. 2023): LLM judges tend to prefer longer responses, independent of

   quality ("verbosity bias").


· Measured E ect -- "repetitive list" attack: an answer is padded by rephrasing and repeating

   its own list items, adding no information. Judge failure rate (padded version preferred), Table 3:


· Claude-v1 91.3% · GPT-3.5 91.3% · GPT-4 8.7% (on 23 answers).


· Root Cause: Long responses look more thorough; the judge con ates verbosity with quality.

   Stronger judges resist better but not perfectly.


· Real-World Impact: Models can game evaluation by adding ller content ("Certainly! Great

   question! Let me explain step-by-step...") to in ate scores.


· Detection: a length-vs-score correlation across models is only a hint -- stronger models also

   write longer. The clean test is the controlled one above: same content, more words.


· Mitigation: compare content-matched pairs, or apply a speci ed statistical length control

(AlpacaEval-LC regresses length out; dividing a score by token count is not the same thing).

Rubric instructions help but do not remove the bias.                                                    39

Benchmark Saturation & Data Contamination

· Saturation: as strong systems approach the ceiling, a benchmark may lose

   discriminating power -- but only an uncertainty estimate can show that two
   scores are actually indistinguishable.


· Contamination: Test items or close variants may appear in training data.


· Overlap Check: Search for exact and near-duplicate benchmark text in known

   training corpora.


· Statistical Clues: Unusually low perplexity can motivate investigation, but is not

   proof.


· Protection: Use private hold-outs, fresh items, and controlled release

   schedules.


· Reporting: Document data cuto s, tool use, and suspected exposure.

· Three Failure Modes of Classic Benchmarks:
   · Static: Questions never change  model can memorise them.

   · General: Test broad knowledge, not deep capability  frontier models saturate.

   · Dynamic: Questions updated regularly (LiveBench: monthly) from new sources (news,

   · Ground-truth veri able: Answers checked programmatically (math, unit tests), not by judge.

Key Takeaways ­ Five Things to Remember

· 1. Data quality is the hidden variable in scaling laws. Chinchilla showed models were data-

   starved (~20 tokens/parameter in its regime); FineWeb-Edu showed those tokens must be
   clean. Garbage In, Garbage Out at any scale.


· 2. Every lter is a precision­recall trade-o . Cheap heuristics rst (Gopher  C4  PPL)

   then expensive neural classi ers. Perplexity ltering can inadvertently erase rare educational
   content.


· 3. BERTScore ranks paraphrases correctly where BLEU does not. The × token

   similarity matrix compares meaning rather than surface form; IDF weighting focuses on
   informative words. It is still not a factual-correctness check, and its scores are not comparable
   to BLEU's.


· 4. LLM judges are fast but biased. Length bias and positional bias can corrupt rankings.

   Mitigation: position swapping, rubric anchoring, multi-judge ensembles.


· 5. Static benchmarks need controls, not abandonment. Use contamination checks, held-

   out or refreshed sets, capability-speci c tasks, uncertainty estimates, and human evaluation
   where appropriate.
